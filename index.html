    Getting started

    1. Open the Simulation tab of Electro Yosr. An example circuit (a 9 V battery driving a red LED) is already running.
    2. Pick a component from the list on the left. Click the board to place it. Press R before clicking to rotate it.
    3. Choose the Wire tool. Click a terminal, then click where the wire should end. Wires bend at a right angle. A wire stops when it reaches another terminal; press Esc to stop it anywhere.
    4. Add a Ground (0 V reference). Without one, the battery's negative terminal is used as 0 V.
    5. The circuit runs live. Click a switch to open or close it. Select any part to change its value and read its voltage, current and power on the right.
    6. Hollow orange circles mark terminals that are not connected to anything. Fix them before trusting a result.

    Measuring

    Place an ammeter in series (the current must pass through it) and a voltmeter in parallel (across the part you are measuring). Readings appear next to each meter.

    Every meter is also drawn on the oscilloscope under the board, over the last 10 seconds. Load the RC charging example, close K1 and watch the capacitor voltage rise along its exponential curve.

    Hover over any wire to see the voltage of that node, measured from ground, at the bottom right of the board.

    Logic circuits

    Logic gates use 5 V logic: a voltage above 2.5 V reads as 1, below reads as 0. Unconnected inputs read as 0. Gates are drawn with IEC rectangular symbols: & for AND, ≥1 for OR, =1 for XOR, and a small circle for inversion.

    AC and analog

    Alternating voltage

    The AC / function generator gives a sine, square or triangle wave. Set its peak voltage Û and frequency f (mains: 325 V peak, 50 Hz, which is 230 V RMS). Choose the oscilloscope span under the board (50 ms shows 2.5 periods at 50 Hz). Put a voltmeter or ammeter in AC (RMS) mode to read U = Û / √2.

    With AC sources the simulator automatically uses a smaller time step (40 steps per period). With very high frequencies it runs in slow motion so that every period is still calculated.

    Power supply chain

    Transformer (set the primary and secondary RMS voltages) → diode bridge → electrolytic capacitor → 78xx regulator. Load the Bridge rectifier with filter example and watch the ripple on the oscilloscope.

    Other parts

    • Op-amp: ideal amplifier with a gain of 100 000 and an output that saturates at ±Vsat. The supply pins are not drawn. Load the Op-amp non-inverting amplifier example.
    • Coil, Zener diode, PNP and N-channel MOSFET transistors, thyristor, triac, optocoupler, relay: see the Library for their formulas and pins.
    • Sensors: move the Light slider of the LDR or the Temperature slider of the thermistor while the circuit runs.
    • Clock: a 0 / 5 V square wave for flip-flops and counters.

    Integrated circuits

    The library has real DIP packages: 74LS00, 74LS02, 74LS04, 74LS08, 74LS32, 74LS86, the CD4511 decoder and the NE555 timer. Pins are numbered as on the datasheet: pin 1 is next to the notch, and numbering goes down the left side and back up the right side.

    Power first

    An integrated circuit works only when it is powered. Connect VCC (pin 14 on the 74LS chips) to +5 V and GND (pin 7) to ground. The +5 V supply symbol in Sources saves wiring. Until the chip is powered, its outputs are disconnected and a notice tells you which pins to wire.

    Rules used by the simulator

    • An input reads 1 when its voltage is above half the supply (2.5 V at 5 V).
    • An unconnected input reads 0. On a real 74LS chip an open input usually reads 1, so always connect unused inputs.
    • CMOS chips (CD4xxx) accept 3 to 15 V; TTL chips (74LS) and the PIC need 5 V.
    • Flip-flops and counters change state on the clock edge shown in the table. The simulator checks the clock at each time step, so clocks up to a few hundred hertz are seen reliably.
    • Outputs have about 100 Ω of internal resistance. Above 25 mA you get a warning: add a series resistor.
    • CD4511: LT and BI are pulled to 1 if left open, and LE to 0, so the decoder works with only A, B, C, D wired.
    • NE555: RESET (pin 4) is pulled to +5 V if left open. Load the NE555 astable example to see the capacitor voltage on the oscilloscope.

    Programming the PIC

    1. Place a PIC16F876A or PIC16F84A from the Programmable group.
    2. Connect VDD to +5 V and VSS to ground (PIC16F876A: pin 20 and pin 8 or 19; PIC16F84A: pin 14 and pin 5).
    3. Select the PIC and press Edit program. Write your program or load an example, then press Compile and load.
    4. The program starts as soon as the PIC is powered. Reset restarts it from the beginning, like the MCLR button.

    The MCLR pin and the crystal can stay unconnected in the simulator. On a real board, connect MCLR to +5 V through 10 kΩ, and a 4 MHz crystal with two 22 pF capacitors on OSC1 and OSC2.

    Interrupts and timers

    Write one procedure called interrupt. The PIC calls it when an enabled interrupt flag is set and GIE = 1. Inside, test the flag, then clear it, otherwise the interrupt runs again at once. GIE is turned off during the routine and back on at the end, as on the real chip.

    LCD library

    Place an LCD 16×2, connect VSS to ground, VDD to +5 V and RW to ground. Declare the six pins with sbit at exactly as wired, then use Lcd_Init, Lcd_Out(row, col, text), Lcd_Out_Cp, Lcd_Chr, Lcd_Chr_Cp, Lcd_Cmd. Commands: _LCD_CLEAR, _LCD_RETURN_HOME, _LCD_CURSOR_OFF, _LCD_UNDERLINE_ON, _LCD_BLINK_CURSOR_ON, _LCD_MOVE_CURSOR_LEFT, _LCD_MOVE_CURSOR_RIGHT, _LCD_SHIFT_LEFT, _LCD_SHIFT_RIGHT, _LCD_TURN_ON, _LCD_TURN_OFF, _LCD_FIRST_ROW, _LCD_SECOND_ROW. If the wiring does not match the declarations, the LCD stays blank and a notice lists the pins the program expects.

    UART library

    Place a Virtual terminal. Wire the PIC's TX (RC6) to the terminal's RXD and the terminal's TXD to RX (RC7). Use UART1_Init(9600), UART1_Write(byte), UART1_Write_Text(text), UART1_Data_Ready, UART1_Read. Select the terminal to see everything it received and to type text back. The receive buffer holds 2 bytes like the real PIC: if the program does not read fast enough, extra bytes are lost and RCSTA.OERR is set.

    Timing and limits

    • Each statement takes about 1 µs. Delay_ms is exact, and an interrupt that runs during a delay makes the delay longer, as on the real chip.
    • The circuit is recalculated every 1 ms. Changes faster than that are not visible on LEDs, and the RB0/INT and RB-change inputs are checked once per millisecond.
    • real numbers use 32-bit precision (about 7 digits), like MikroPascal. FloatToStr writes up to 7 significant digits.
    • ADC_Read(n) returns 0 to 1023 for 0 to 5 V on AN0 to AN4 (RA0, RA1, RA2, RA3, RA5).
    • Not supported yet: PWM (CCP), EEPROM, I²C and SPI, custom LCD characters, Timer1 external clock, records, pointers and var parameters.

    How it calculates

    The simulator uses nodal analysis, the same method professional tools like SPICE use. Every group of connected terminals is a node. It writes Kirchhoff's current law at each node and solves the system of equations for the node voltages.

    Diodes, LEDs and transistors are not linear (their current follows an exponential law), so it solves the system several times per step with Newton's method until the answer stops changing.

    Time advances in steps of 1 ms. Capacitors use the backward Euler method: at each step a capacitor acts like a resistor C/Δt plus a current source that remembers its previous voltage. That is why the RC curve on the oscilloscope matches the theory.

    Limits of the model

    • Values are close to real parts but simplified. Lamps and motors are fixed resistances; a real filament's resistance rises when hot.
    • Batteries have a 0.5 Ω internal resistance by default, so a short circuit gives a large but finite current. The simulator warns you above 3 A.
    • An LED burns out above 50 mA, a lamp above 1.8× its rated power, and a fuse above its rating. Press Reset to replace them.
    • Logic gates are ideal with a 10 Ω output and switch within one 1 ms step. There is no supply pin to wire.
    • Only DC sources are available in this version (no AC generator or inductor).
    • The PIC runs MikroPascal interpreted statement by statement, with timers and interrupts modelled. It is not a cycle-exact PIC instruction set simulator.
